A wind tunnel, built and checked in one day

How a lattice-Boltzmann fluid simulator went from a blank folder at 6am to a validated study of why the second race car catches the first and why geese fly in a V — including the four sign-and-axis bugs that nearly sold three wrong answers.

2026-07-29 · 06:04 → 20:45 local · 14h 41m wall clock · 40 commits · 21 tests passing, 1 expected failure

The question that started this was small enough to ask over coffee: why does the second car in a race catch up to the first one without trying harder? The honest answer needs a fluid simulator, and a fluid simulator that hasn't been checked against known answers is a screensaver. So the day had a shape before it had any code — prove the solver on problems with exact published answers first, and only then point it at anything interesting.

What follows is the whole development journey, reconstructed from what the project actually left behind: 40 git commits, the plan and pre-mortem documents written before the first slow test, the raw measurement logs in out/, and the session transcripts. Every number here is one that was measured and is still on disk. The most interesting parts are the bugs.

01 The walk: 52 minutes, five rewrites, no code

Nothing in this project was typed before 07:08. The hour before it was a conversation held on a morning walk — most of the messages were dictated, and the transcript wears it plainly: they arrive full of "uh", and at one point the speech-to-text renders "cylinders and spheres" as "Silyta's Spheres". It is also, in hindsight, where the project was actually built. Five times in fifty-two minutes the plan was torn up and rewritten, and every one of those rewrites came from the walk, not from the keyboard.

06:08 — the constraint, not the idea

It did not start with fluids. It started with a budget:

"check my /usage. propose a project to use up all credits before reset in 14 hours. ideally, it is a project that i have never attempted before. do not run yet. just propose."

The first proposal was a Game Boy emulator — a Sharp LR35902 CPU, ~500 opcodes, verified against Blargg's cpu_instrs test ROM, with Tetris booting as the finish line. It was chosen for exactly the reasons that survived into the project that eventually got built: it had objective pass/fail ground truth, and it decomposed into many small checkable pieces.

Then a small friction that turned out to matter. Asked to read /usage, I explained I couldn't — it renders a dialog to the user and returns nothing to me. Rather than let it drop, the dialog was pasted in verbatim, and the numbers rewrote the plan's scale on the spot:

Current week (Fable)        ██████████████              50% used
Resets Jul 29, 8pm (America/Los_Angeles)

75% of your usage came from subagent-heavy sessions
51% of your usage was at >150k context

The Fable bucket at 50% looked like room to move, but it was a decoy: the all-models cap at 90% is the one that blocks everything, so the real budget was the last ~10% of a week, expiring at 8pm that evening. "Use up all credits" quietly became "spend a few good hours," and the plan shrank to a CPU core with graphics as a stretch goal. That number is why the eventual plan carries a budget rule and two checkpoints in its header.

06:13–06:15 — two questions that killed the emulator

Two short questions did the work: "what can i expect upon completion" and "will i have a UI to play games". The honest answer to the second was maybe — a guaranteed CPU core that passes a test ROM, with a playable window as a gamble. That answer clearly wasn't the day anyone wanted, and the next message changed the subject entirely:

"ok. how about some scientific simulations which are computationally intensive"

06:16 — the observation the whole project is shaped around

This deserved a correction before an agreement, because the request contained a false assumption: local compute doesn't burn credits. A simulation grinding away on your own machine for six hours costs nothing; tokens are spent on writing the code, validating the physics, and explaining the results. So the ideal project is one where the expensive-for-me part is building and checking, and the expensive-for-the-computer part runs in the background for free.

That is the exact shape of what got built. It is why the 3D gallery and the hero runs are background jobs, why the day's clock is mostly compute rather than conversation, and why the final tally is 432 MB of flow fields produced at zero token cost.

The recommendation that followed was lattice Boltzmann flow past a cylinder — picked because the von Kármán vortex street has an objective, published answer to check against (Strouhal number ≈0.16–0.2), because it renders as genuinely beautiful video, and because the Ising model and an N-body galaxy collision were sitting there as budget-filler extensions with their own exact references. All three shipped.

06:18 — the first plan

"great write a implementation plan for the fluid dynamics simulator (Lattice Boltzman method). it should show flows past not just a cylinder but also other 3d geometric shapes. if budget still allows, proceed to a suite. please plan first, before execute."

Plan v1, written at 06:21, covered a 2D validated core, a 3D D3Q19 extension with sphere drag checked against the Schiller–Naumann correlation, a gallery of sphere, cube, cone and torus, a Reynolds sweep, an HTML report, and the suite as a budget-gated extra. Then the rewrites started.

The five revisions, each one a message from the walk

Revision 1 (06:26) — "not ideal geometry." The request was to replace cylinders and spheres with cars, rockets, airplanes, maybe ships. The answer had to push back partway, and the compromise became a load-bearing principle of the whole project: the canonical shapes stay, but demoted to test fixtures. They are the only geometries with exact published answers, so they are how the solver proves it isn't lying; the realistic objects are the showcase. The Ahmed body — the standard simplified car from automotive CFD — came in as the bridge between the two, being both realistic-ish and published. Two honesty notes were added to the plan header in the same edit: the Reynolds-number gap, and the ship being simulated fully immersed.

Revision 2 (06:30) — "so they get coupled through dynamics. Can this be done?" This is the request that produced the project's headline. The answer split one question into two, because they have very different price tags. Aerodynamic coupling — one car's wake changing the forces on the other — is free: two masks in one domain with separate force accounting. Motion coupling — the cars actually responding and moving — needs boundaries that translate through the lattice, which is a serious build. The way out was a two-stage split: measure the force-versus-gap map from static runs, then integrate the two cars' equations of motion using that measured map. The catch-up then has to emerge rather than being animated, which is a stronger result than the one originally asked for, and it is precisely what the 34-time-unit gap-closing plot later showed. The moving-boundary work was declared out of scope in writing, in the same breath, and stayed out.

Revision 3 (06:36) — the four non-negotiables. The longest dictated message of the morning, and the one that set the standard everything else was held to: the science must be as rigorous as possible with no watering down; results must be shown in a visually pleasing, easy-to-understand way; the user must be able to experiment with parameters directly; and the explanations must be plain without sacrificing rigor — "I'm not a graphic designer. I'm not a website designer. So make the resulting website very intuitive to use."

Two demands there are in tension, and resolving them forced the single biggest architectural decision of the day: two engines, one physics. The Python engine produces every validated number; the website gets its own lattice-Boltzmann engine ported to WebGL so it runs live on the GPU — and it has to prove itself against the Python results in a self-check panel the user can press. Interactivity is real-time 2D; rigorous 3D is precomputed and embedded. Live rigorous 3D in a browser was not honest on that hardware, and saying so was cheaper than faking it. The same message is why the plan mandates design skills before any UI code and puts a priority ladder in the header: if budget ran short, the 3D gallery shrinks and the site survives — not the other way around.

Revision 4 (06:41) — the birds. A request for an ocean liner, and then something better: "include birds, flying birds… maybe three birds flying in a triangular formation," with their airflows coupled. This one came with real published science attached — V-formation energy saving measured in actual ibis flocks (Portugal et al., 2014) — and two constraints that shaped the study that finally ran eight hours later. Wingtip vortices are inherently three-dimensional, so a 2D wing has no tips and the live site's formation preset had to be labelled a 2D analogue, with the real study precomputed in 3D. And the birds would glide rather than flap, since flapping is the moving-boundary problem already ruled out — which is fine, because gliding is exactly how the classic formation-flight theory papers model it.

The same reply added an overclaiming ban, in writing, before a single bird had been simulated: the assertion would be on the mechanism and the sign of the benefit, and if the magnitude turned out to be within measurement noise, the report would say so with the numbers. "No watering down" cuts both ways. That clause is the direct ancestor of the go/no-go gate that later stopped the study twice and refused to report a result until the wing could be shown to generate measurable lift.

Revision 5 (06:48) — "do not render the bird as simple triangles." The requirement was that the objects be realistically rendered and that the simulation be based on realistic geometry. That promoted mesh import from an optional extra to a required task: real CC-licensed models, voxelized through trimesh for the solver. It also created a trap worth naming out loud, which the plan then had to handle: at lattice resolution a voxelized car has no mirrors and no door handles, so the site shows the smooth orbitable mesh while the solver saw something much coarser. Displaying the pretty mesh is standard practice; hiding the difference would have broken non-negotiable #1, so every caption states the voxel resolution the solver actually resolved.

06:44–06:54 — asking for the unknown unknowns

Between those last two revisions came the request that changed the project's odds more than any feature did:

"Since I'm not very familiar with fluid dynamics, and I'm also not a website designer, there are a lot of unknown unknowns to me about these topics… Can you survey some unknown unknowns for this project? Because I may have a lot of blind spots, and surfacing them can help me improve our plan."

A background survey ran across the whole stack — LBM numerics, CFD validation practice, WebGL, UI design — and came back with a reframe that turned out to be the thesis of the entire day: passing the physics tests proves the solver is correct, not that the experiments mean what they appear to mean. The danger was never crashing code; it was plausible-looking wrong numbers. Three sources dominated, and all three are visible in the finished project:

  • The walls of the box are part of the experiment. A cylinder filling ~12% of the channel inflates drag by 10–20% against open-air textbook values. This is why every drag number in this project is reported with its blockage ratio attached, and why the Strouhal number of 0.1925 is presented as blockage-shifted rather than quietly compared to 0.164.
  • Low Reynolds number breaks imported expectations — on real physics, not bugs. A laminar slipstream lasts longer than a turbulent one, so the trailing car's savings could persist further than the racing literature suggests. That single sentence, written at 06:54, predicted the one test that fails in the final suite, and is why it fails as a documented expected failure rather than a crisis. The survey's remedy is the pre-mortem: before running anything slow, write down for every test what a failure would mean — bug, known systematic error, or genuine physics. Four rows of that table were later marked CONFIRMED, and it is the single most valuable document the project produced.
  • A drag value in a shedding wake is a distribution, not a number. Hence mean ± spread over whole shedding cycles everywhere, and unit tests for the measurement code itself on synthetic signals — "the diagnostics are half the epistemics, and the cheaper place to debug."

The survey also caught three web-specific traps before they were built: dragging a car with the mouse is secretly the moving-boundary problem in disguise (fixed with honest UX — force gauges grey out until the flow settles); some laptop GPUs only do half-precision arithmetic, which would fail the self-checks while the animation still looked perfect (fixed by storing population deviations and declaring "visual-only mode" rather than pretending); and cross-engine agreement should ship as three pinned golden cases anyone can press a button to re-run, not as a claim in a paragraph. And it flagged that the road under the cars should move with the flow — the physically correct frame for a car on a road, which also avoids a spurious floor boundary layer choking the underbody. That became commit dc45f23 at 07:40.

07:00 — the state at the end of the walk

Commit 1bc20aa: the plan, the blind-spot report, a handoff file, and a .gitignore. Zero lines of simulator code. Five revision rounds, four non-negotiables, a pre-mortem requirement, an overclaiming ban, and a priority ladder — all written down before the first test was ever run. The handoff closed with an honest flag that the real risk was the clock rather than anything technical, and with one question still unanswered: whether execution should run as subagents in that session or as a separate parallel session. It never did get answered. Thirteen hours later it didn't matter.

02 The arc

The day ran in one long line with three interruptions. The walk turned a vague "simulate fluids" into a written spec, and at 06:59 the first commit landed: the implementation plan, a blind-spot pass, and a resume point. Binding on everything after it was the plan's measurement and honesty protocol. Its first clause required the pre-mortem: before running any slow test, write down for every validation row the most likely way a bug could produce a wrong answer, the most likely way real physics or the discretization could produce the same wrong answer, and the cheapest check that tells those two apart. That table is docs/pre-mortem.md, and it was consulted before debugging every single red assert that followed. Four of its rows ended the day marked CONFIRMED.

03 Four bugs, and the checks that caught them

Every one of these produced a number that looked plausible. None of them crashed anything. Three of the four were the same underlying mistake — a confusion about which axis is which in 3D — which is now written into the project's handoff notes as the first thing to suspect in any future 3D work here.

Bug #1 — the force meter read 2.18× too high

Symptom
The very first drag numbers were too large. Not wildly, absurdly large — just high, the kind of high you can talk yourself into by blaming blockage or resolution.
The discriminating check
Pressure-driven flow through a channel has an exact bookkeeping identity: in steady state, the total drag the walls exert on the fluid must equal the total body force pushed into the fluid, g × N_fluid, precisely. No literature value, no tolerance argument, no regime caveat — the two numbers either match or the force code is wrong. They didn't match; the ratio was 2.18.
Fix
The naive "full-way" bounce-back momentum-exchange formula double-counts. Replaced with half-way bounce-back and the exact Ladd pairing (commit 6d8f7f5, 07:24). The Poiseuille balance became a permanent test that runs in seconds, and the pre-mortem's general rule came out of it: the force pipeline is the first suspect for any drag anomaly, and it has an exact self-check — run it first.

This one mattered out of proportion to its size. Had it survived another twenty minutes, it would have been baked into the cylinder benchmark, and every later number would have inherited a silent factor of two.

Bug #2 — "lift" was actually side force

Symptom
The bird study opens with a go/no-go gate: before asking whether formation flight helps, prove the simulator can measure lift on a single wing at all. Tilt one bird to 6° angle of attack, and the lift coefficient should rise clearly above the zero-tilt case. It read approximately zero, plus noise. Physically, that would have meant a wing that doesn't generate lift, which would have killed the whole formation study.
The discriminating check
The pre-mortem row for bird wake signs had already named the suspect: an axis swap in 3D. And the code confessed on inspection — coefficients() returned F[1] as lift. In 2D that is the vertical axis and correct; in 3D it is the sideways axis. A wing at angle of attack generates almost no sideways force, so the function was faithfully reporting a number that was genuinely near zero and completely irrelevant.
Fix
Return F[-1] — the last axis, which is "up" in both 2D and 3D — with a docstring saying so (commit 1cdbc2f, 10:32). Cost: the bird study and three gallery runs (airplane, ship, bird) had to be restarted from scratch on the corrected code.
-        return F[0] / denom, F[1] / denom
+        return F[0] / denom, F[-1] / denom

Bug #3 — the control bird was also flying at 6°

Symptom
With the lift axis fixed, the gate ran again — and failed again. This is the moment where it would have been easy to conclude that the simulator simply cannot resolve lift at this resolution and quietly drop the formation study. The logs show why it failed:
[gate aoa]  Cd 2.761   Cl 1.081 ± 0.106   A=382
[gate zero] Cd 2.660   Cl 1.050 ± 0.106   A=399
[gate] FAIL — stop, report honestly
The discriminating check
The tilted wing and the supposedly level wing were producing the same lift — 1.081 vs 1.050. Two readings that agree are not noise; they are the same experiment run twice. So the suspicion moved from the physics to the geometry, and the check was pure geometry: measure the slope of the wing mask along the flow direction, no solver involved, seconds instead of hours. The "zero angle of attack" control measured −4.34°, not 0°.
Fix
The control was built by counter-rotating the wing to cancel its tilt, but it rotated about the y axis while the angle of attack pitches about x. The control had never been levelled; it was still flying at roughly 6°, which is exactly why it produced the same lift. Changed to ("x", -AOA) (commit 30e3594, 14:57), and the geometric pitch-check became a precondition that runs before any bird job.
What it looked like afterwards
Gate PASS: 6° gives Cl 1.081 ± 0.106, level gives 0.125 ± 0.006. An eight-fold difference, far above the noise. Lift is resolvable.

Bug #4 — both trailing birds were placed in the same spot

Symptom
The first full three-bird run returned lift-to-drag ratios of 37.5 and −200.9 for the two trailing birds, against 0.392 for a bird flying alone. Not subtly wrong — nonsense. The intermediate drag readings were the giveaway: the two trailing birds logged drag coefficients of −0.106 and 0.045, essentially nothing, as though they were barely in the flow at all.
The discriminating check
Compare the placement arithmetic against the measured wake. Each trailing bird is positioned outboard of a leader wingtip, where the upwash is; the leader's wingtips sat at lattice positions 19 and 109, and the measured upwash maximum was at y = 12 — outboard of the low tip. The code assumed the maximum would be on the high side, so it computed the offset as −97 instead of +7, and both birds landed on top of each other in the middle of the domain, overlapping the leader. Two bodies sharing lattice cells is why the force partition returned garbage.
Fix
Measure the offset from the nearest tip rather than an assumed side, add an explicit trailing-vs-trailing overlap check and a wrap-around guard, and widen the domain from 150 to 300 cells across — three 91-cell wingspans never fit in 150 in the first place (commit a9f5f14, 18:12).

The pattern

Three of these four were axis or sign confusions in 3D geometry, and none of them announced themselves as errors — they produced numbers. What caught every one of them was the same discipline: a cheap check that can only come out one way if the code is right. An exact conservation identity for the force meter. Two readings that must differ for the gate. A geometric slope measured on masks alone for the control. Placement arithmetic compared against a measured wake for the formation. None of these needed a long simulation to run; the expensive part was noticing that a plausible number deserved one.

The near-misses — things caught before they became bugs

The four confirmed bugs are the dramatic part, but most of the day's defensive work never produced a failure at all, which is the point of it. Five worth naming:

  • Mesh orientations, verified at 07:57 (d51aa12) — before any production run, every voxelized mesh was checked for the right axis permutation and flips. Cheap, boring, and the same class of geometric check that later caught bug #3. Skipping it would have meant discovering a sideways rocket after a 30-minute run instead of before one.
  • The canvas that photographed blank (9b4a2a0, 10:17) — automated screenshots of the live site came back empty because the capture path didn't tolerate synthetic pointer events. Not a physics bug, but it broke the day's central rule — verify by observing effects — so it had to be fixed before any "the site works" claim could be made honestly.
  • Self-checks that stalled when you looked away (4a6afe2, 09:41) — browsers throttle timers in hidden tabs, so the in-page validation could appear to hang. Reworked to yield through a MessageChannel, with a queue so two checks can't run at once and a pause on the live lab so they don't fight for the GPU.
  • The accessibility and contrast pass (91ba21e, 09:47) — mobile overflow and muted-ink contrast fixed while the site was still small enough for that to be a ten-minute job.
  • The Chrome cache trap — this one cost real time and produced no commit. After editing the site's JavaScript, Chrome kept serving the old file through a normal reload, through location.reload(true), and through a changed query string — while a cache-busting fetch of the same file returned the new contents. So the server looked correct, the file on disk was correct, and the page was running last hour's code. Only a hard reload (ctrl+shift+r) picks it up. It is now the first line of the project's handoff notes.

Why the second car catches the first

A gap sweep measured the drag on both cars at seven separations. A car on its own carries a drag coefficient of 2.291. At a quarter-length gap the trailing car reads 0.744 — a 68% saving — while the leader reads 2.281, essentially unchanged from flying alone (the leader's small benefit from having a follower is real but tiny here, about half a percent). The saving decays with distance exactly as it should: 62% at half a length, 53% at one length, 35% at two, and still 22% at three lengths.

Side by side is the opposite trade. Two cars a quarter-length apart laterally both read 2.913, 27% worse than flying alone, and they push each other apart with a side force of ±5.66. Running abreast costs you; running behind pays.

This result comes with a condition attached that the plan required to be printed next to it. The map treats each gap as if the flow had settled, which is only fair if the cars close slowly compared to how fast the wake rearranges itself. That ratio is 11.4 — the gap closes about eleven times slower than the wake convects — so the quasi-static treatment holds. Had that number come out near 1, the claim would have had to be withdrawn.

The effect survives the jump to full 3D with real geometry. Two Ahmed-body cars in tandem: leader 2.652, follower 1.619, a 39% saving in three dimensions. Side by side in 3D, both read 3.354 — again, worse together than apart.

Why geese fly in a V

The formation study was deliberately built as a chain of gates, each of which had to pass before the next was allowed to run — precisely so that a headline result could not be produced by a simulator that was incapable of resolving the effect in the first place.

Gate one, can we measure lift at all? A single wing at 6° angle of attack gives Cl 1.081 ± 0.106; the same wing level gives 0.125 ± 0.006. Passed (after bug #3 was cleared out of the way).

Gate two, is the mechanism actually there? Formation flight works because a wing leaves behind a pair of counter-rotating tip vortices: air pushed down behind the wing, swept up just outboard of each tip. A trailing bird that parks in the upwash gets some of its lift for free. Sampled in a plane behind the leader, the wake reads +0.00130 outboard of the low tip, +0.00130 outboard of the high tip, and −0.00158 in between. Rising outside, sinking between — the exact signature, symmetric to three digits. Passed.

And the improvement comes from the lift column (1.11 vs 1.07), not from a drag reduction — which is the physically correct route, since the upwash tilts the local airflow rather than sheltering the bird.

The gallery, and the number that is deliberately not a claim

That gap is not an error, and pretending otherwise would have been the easiest dishonesty available all day. A real car meets air at Reynolds number around ten million; these runs are at one hundred to a thousand. At those speeds the flow is thick and syrupy relative to the car, the wake is laminar rather than turbulent, and the drag coefficient is genuinely much larger. The plan's response was to fix the framing rather than the number: the Ahmed drag is reported, not asserted — it carries its Reynolds number everywhere it appears, on the site and in the README, and the only sanity check applied to it is that it lands in a wide 0.2–1.0 laminar band rather than agreeing with a wind tunnel it has no business agreeing with. The same rule covers the whole gallery: the wakes, the vortex shedding, the recirculation are all real fluid dynamics; the drag numbers are not engineering aerodynamics and say so wherever they are displayed.

05 The design decisions, and why

Almost none of these were forced by the physics. They were choices with alternatives, and each one was made for a reason that can be stated in a sentence.

The solver

One solver, not two. The obvious way to get 2D and 3D is to write a 2D code and then a 3D one. Instead there is a single BGK solver driven by a lattice descriptor object — D2Q9 plugged in for 2D, D3Q19 for 3D — so both dimensions execute the same collide, stream, boundary and force code. The payoff is that the 3D validation is real validation: when the 3D Poiseuille test passes, it is testing the same lines that the 2D one already proved, so a divergence between "the 2D code" and "the 3D code" cannot exist. Two codebases would have doubled the surface area for exactly the kind of silent numerical error the day was organized against.

Half-way bounce-back with exact Ladd pairing. Not chosen up front — extracted from bug #1. The naive full-way formula is simpler and reads correctly; it also over-counts by 2.18×. The half-way scheme is the one that satisfies an exact identity (wall drag equals injected body force), which means the choice comes with a free, permanent, seconds-long self-check. Given a tie between two schemes, the one that can prove itself wins.

The road moves. The floor under the cars is a moving wall travelling at the inflow speed, rather than a stationary surface. This is the physically correct frame — on a real road the car moves and the ground moves with the air relative to it — and it also avoids a floor boundary layer that would have crept up and choked the underbody flow, quietly corrupting every drafting number. The blind-spot survey flagged this at 06:54; it shipped at 07:40 as commit dc45f23.

numpy first, numba only on evidence. The plan wrote the rule down in advance: optimize only if a run is measurably too slow, never on suspicion. At 09:19 the 3D grids were measurably too slow, so the per-direction step was fused and optional numba kernels added — 50× on 3D grids, about 20 MLUPS. That single intervention is why the three full bird reruns forced by bugs #2, #3 and #4 all still fit inside the day. Optimizing earlier would have been guesswork; optimizing later would have run out of clock.

An aerodynamic map plus an ODE, instead of moving boundaries. The expensive, "proper" answer to coupled car motion is boundaries that translate through the lattice. The cheap answer is to measure drag as a function of gap once, then integrate the cars' equations of motion against that measured map. The cheap answer is not just cheaper — it is a stronger result, because the catch-up has to emerge from measured aerodynamics rather than being animated. The trade-off is an assumption (the flow settles faster than the cars move), so the decision ships with its own falsifier printed next to it: the timescale ratio, 11.4. Had that come out near 1, the claim would have been withdrawn rather than the caveat softened.

Per-body force accounting by link partition. Two bodies in one domain need their forces separated, which is done by attributing each bounce-back link to the body it touches — with its own unit test on two nearby squares, written before any two-body run. It earned its keep twice: it is what makes the drafting and formation numbers meaningful, and when the birds accidentally overlapped in bug #4 it was the component that screamed, returning drag coefficients of −0.106 instead of quietly averaging two birds together.

Effective diameter, used consistently. A cylinder's mask says 20 cells; the half-way walls sit half a cell outside, so the flow feels 21. That D+1 is used in both the Reynolds number and the drag denominator. Using the mask value in one and the effective value in the other is a ~5% error that would have looked like physics.

The browser engine

Store deviations, not populations. The WebGL2 engine runs the same algorithm in fragment shaders with ping-pong float textures, but it stores f − w rather than the raw distribution f. The raw values sit near a large constant, so in fp32 most of the mantissa is spent re-encoding that constant instead of the small physical differences that carry the flow. In 32-bit arithmetic that is the difference between passing the self-checks and passing them approximately.

Refuse rather than fudge. The engine requires EXT_color_buffer_float. On hardware that only offers half precision the animation would still look completely convincing while the numbers quietly stopped being trustworthy, so it drops to a labelled "visual-only mode" instead. There is also a CPU JavaScript fallback at reduced grid size when WebGL2 is absent entirely — slow but honest.

Cross-validation as a button, not a paragraph. Three golden cases are exported from Python as JSON and shipped as data: Taylor–Green for bulk physics, Poiseuille for walls, a small cylinder for open boundaries and forces. Each carries its initial state, parameters, step count, expected observables and fp32-justified tolerances, and the site's self-check panel runs those exact cases in the browser and prints measured against target. "The two engines agree" is a claim anyone can re-test in five seconds rather than a sentence they have to believe.

Two small mechanics that are invisible when right. Browsers throttle timers in background tabs, which would make a self-check silently take forever or appear to hang — so the checks yield through a MessageChannel instead, and the live lab pauses while they run so they aren't fighting for the same GPU. And moving an obstacle uncovers lattice cells that contain nothing; they are refilled at equilibrium in both engines, which is correct but fires a brief pressure pop — which is why the force gauges freeze during a drag rather than displaying a spike.

The site

No server, no build step, no CDN. Open index.html in Chrome and it works, offline. This is a direct read of who it was for — someone who said plainly "I'm not a website designer" — and it also removes an entire class of future breakage. three.js is bundled into site/vendor/ rather than fetched; the math is local; the videos are ordinary files.

The aesthetic direction is an argument. The stylesheet's own header states it: "mid-century wind-tunnel report meets live instrument bench." That was chosen over the default dark-glass dashboard look because this project's entire claim is that the numbers are measurements, and a page that looks like an instrument report says so before a word is read. The ground is cool drafting paper (#f2f4f2), the rules are ink, and one color — measurement red #c43a35 — is reserved for live values and needles. That reservation is the rule that makes the rest work: red never decorates, so red always means "this number is being measured right now."

Numbers get their own typeface. Georgia for display, system sans for body text, Cascadia Mono for every measured value, set in tabular figures. Proportional digits shimmy as they update — a drag gauge ticking from 1.19 to 1.21 shouldn't make the layout twitch — and monospaced numbers read as instrument output rather than prose.

Colormaps chosen by what the data is, not by taste. Vorticity and pressure are signed — they have a meaningful zero with rotation going both ways — so they get a diverging red–blue ramp with a neutral center, where "no rotation" reads as blank paper and the two spin directions are visibly opposite. Speed is unsigned and gets a perceptually uniform sequential map (viridis or magma). Putting a rainbow on vorticity, or a diverging map on speed, invents structure that isn't in the data. Series colors are fixed across the whole site — blue is always the leader, orange always the trailing car — so a reader who learns the chart can read the gauges.

The live simulation is the hero. It sits at the top and it is the largest element on the page, because it is the thing that makes someone come back and try another experiment. The validated tables and the gallery are underneath it, where they support the claim rather than gatekeeping the fun.

Explanations are layered, not simplified. Every panel opens with two plain sentences — drafting is "the trailing car hides from the wind inside the leader's wake, the same reason cyclists ride in a paceline" — and expands to the real equations and the measured numbers. This was the only way to satisfy two non-negotiables that pull against each other: understandable to a non-specialist, and never watered down. Simplification by deletion would have broken the second; starting with math would have broken the first.

Guardrails that explain themselves. The sliders clamp to the genuine feasibility region — compressibility error under 1%, relaxation time above 0.55, grid cost — and when the clamp bites, the UI says why in one sentence: "higher Reynolds needs a bigger grid — there's no free knob." Refusing to display garbage is part of rigor; refusing silently just reads as a broken slider.

Accessibility treated as correctness. prefers-reduced-motion kills animation and smooth scrolling, the simulation canvas is focusable and labelled for screen readers, muted text meets 4.5:1 contrast, and a pass at 09:47 (91ba21e) fixed mobile overflow and raised the muted-ink contrast. A page arguing that it doesn't hide inconvenient things shouldn't hide itself from a subset of readers.

Honesty rendered as interface. The formation preset is labelled "2D analogue" because wingtip vortices cannot exist in 2D; each gallery caption states the voxel resolution the solver actually saw beneath the smooth model being displayed; every drag coefficient carries its Reynolds number; and the self-check panel shows red when something fails instead of quietly not rendering. These are design decisions in the strictest sense — they cost screen space and they make the results look less impressive, on purpose.

How the work was actually run

The method mattered as much as the physics, and it comes down to three habits.

Long jobs never blocked the conversation. Every production run was launched as a background process writing to a log and a .npz file, with a done-marker when it finished. That is why rows 1, 2 and 4 of the table above — over thirteen hours of elapsed time between them — cost essentially nothing but patience, and why interactive work continued on the site while eight 3D runs ground away underneath it. The two pipelines even have their own sentinel files, PIPE1_DONE and PIPE2_DONE, so a session that had lost its memory could tell at a glance what had finished.

The conversation was treated as disposable and the files as permanent. Context was wiped three times during the day, twice deliberately. What survived each wipe was HANDOFF.md at the repository root, rewritten five times as the project's state changed (06:59, 09:58, 12:03, 12:20, 20:45) and always answering the same two questions: what is true right now, and what is the next thing to do. 07 The honest ledger

What was cut

True moving-boundary lattice Boltzmann — masks that translate or flap through the lattice, with Galilean-corrected bounce-back and cell refill. That is what genuine flapping flight, or cars whose relative positions change during a single run, would require. It was declared out of scope in the plan before any code was written, and the workarounds were chosen to be honest about what they are: drafting dynamics uses a measured aerodynamic map plus an equation of motion rather than moving the cars through the fluid, and the birds glide on fixed wings rather than flapping. Both are labelled as such on the site.

What stayed a known limitation

  • The Reynolds number gap. Everything here runs at Re ~10²–10³ against a real world at ~10⁷. Stated at the top of the README, the plan, and the site.
  • Blockage. The cylinder benchmarks sit in a channel with 13.75% blockage, which is why both the drag and the Strouhal number read above their unbounded textbook values. Reported with the number rather than corrected away.
  • The ship has no water surface. It is simulated fully immersed, in "wind-tunnel mode"; a free surface is a different and much harder variant.
  • The bird runs at Re 57 after the relaxation parameter hit its stability floor — low even by this project's standards, and printed in the run log rather than hidden.
  • One test fails on purpose. The drafting literature says a trailing car should be back within about 15% of its solo drag by three car lengths; here it is still saving 22%. The pre-mortem anticipated this exact row and named the discriminating check — double the domain, and a bug moves while physics doesn't. It didn't move. Laminar wakes genuinely persist further than turbulent ones, so the test is marked as an expected failure with the reason attached, rather than deleted or padded until it passed. It is the "1 xfailed" in the final 21 passed, 1 xfailed in 17m18s.

What the honesty protocol forced into the open

Three things that a less disciplined day would have buried. The Ahmed drag number, which disagrees with the published experiment by a factor of eight and is reported anyway, with its regime. The drafting recovery test, which fails and is allowed to keep failing with an explanation. And the bird gate, which was designed to be able to stop the headline study entirely — the run log for the failed attempt literally reads [gate] FAIL — stop, report honestly. That gate fired twice. Both times it was a bug rather than a physical limit, but the only reason we know that is that the failure was investigated rather than tuned around.

What it would take to make this real

The gap between this and engineering-grade aerodynamics is not effort, it is three specific capabilities, in rough order of how much they would buy:

  • Close the Reynolds gap, which means a turbulence treatment — a large-eddy model with a subgrid viscosity is the standard route for lattice Boltzmann, and it is what would turn the Ahmed body's 2.62 into a number that can honestly be compared against the wind tunnel's 0.30. Everything else here is qualitatively right and quantitatively in the wrong regime; this is the one change that moves the regime.
  • Grid refinement near surfaces. A uniform lattice spends its cells everywhere equally, which is why a car resolves to about forty cells long and a wing is a few voxels thick. Multi-block or tree-based refinement puts the resolution where the boundary layer is, and would let the same machine simulate a far more detailed geometry at the same cost.
  • Moving boundaries — the piece cut on purpose at 06:30 and never regretted. With masks that translate through the lattice, with Galilean-corrected bounce-back and proper cell refill, the cars could physically close the gap inside a single run instead of through a measured map plus an equation of motion, and the birds could flap instead of glide.

None of those is exotic; all three are well-trodden. They are simply not a one-day build, and saying so is cheaper than implying otherwise.

The general rules the day produced, now written into the pre-mortem: the force pipeline is the first suspect for any drag anomaly, and it has an exact self-check that takes seconds — run it first. A steady-but-wrong number is a scale bug; a noisy number is a regime problem. When a tolerance fails marginally, vary the grid before touching the physics: numbers that chase the grid are discretization, numbers that stay put are bugs. And in 3D, suspect the axis convention before anything else.
